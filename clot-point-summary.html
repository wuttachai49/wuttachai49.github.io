Overview & how to use this manual

One playbook, three analyzers. Every topic below is tagged by which analyzer it applies to — read straight through before a first install, or jump to a topic when you're standing in front of a machine.

What the CX-series does

The CX-series analyzers perform in-vitro analysis of blood coagulation and fibrinolysis using human plasma. They run three measurement methods on one platform — clotting (PT, APTT, FIB, TT), immunoturbidimetric (D-Dimer, FDP), and chromogenic substrate (Antithrombin III) — so a single instrument can cover routine and special coagulation testing for hospitals from primary-care clinics up to core laboratories.

Who this is for

This is an Application Specialist playbook: the person who installs the analyzer on-site, trains hospital lab staff to run it, and stays the first call when results or hardware don't behave. Every section is written to be taught from — the training curriculum (§08) is sequenced as a session you can actually run at a hospital.

03 · How it measures

Measurement principle

One optical system, three measurement methods, applied to whichever test is running. This is the explanation to give a lab director who asks "how does this actually work" — and the foundation for reading a reaction curve in §04.

MethodTestsWhat's happening physically
Clotting
optical transmission / turbidimetry		 PT, APTT, FIB, TT At the start of the reaction the sample's optical density is very weak. As fibrin clots form, transmitted light decreases and optical density rises, plateauing once the sample is fully coagulated. The analyzer plots this change as a coagulation curve and detects the coagulation end time.
Immunoturbidimetric D-Dimer, FDP Antibody-coated particles bind specifically to the antigen (D-Dimer / FDP) in the sample. Binding increases the turbidity of the reaction system; the analyzer quantifies the measurand from the change in optical density.
Chromogenic substrate Antithrombin III A synthesized compound carries a chromogenic marker that dissociates during the reaction, changing the sample's color. The analyzer calculates activity from the optical density change of monochromatic light passing through the cuvette.

Coagulation Waveform Analysis (CWA)

For the clotting method, the reaction curve isn't just a stopwatch for clot time — its shape carries real coagulation kinetics. Siegemund et al. (2014) proposed reading three curves from the same run, and the CX series builds its calculation methods around exactly this idea (detailed in full in §04):

Original curve

Characterizes fibrinogen content — positively correlated with the amount of cross-linked fibrin polymer generated.

First derivative curve

Fibrin production rate — directly related to thrombin activity. Modeled vs. experimental correlation: R² = 0.95.

Second derivative curve

Fibrin-generation acceleration and thrombin-generation speed — related to prothrombinase [F Xa–F Va–Ca²⁺–PL] activity.

Every clotting-method curve moves through three phases, visible in every diagram in this playbook: a flat baseline period (reagent has just mixed in — factors are activating, but no fibrin has formed yet, so luminous flux barely moves), a steep reaction period (fibrin forms fast, luminous flux drops sharply), and a flat plateau period (fibrinogen is fully converted, no more fibrin forms, signal flattens again). That three-phase shape is what "Z-shaped" means when you read a normal curve check in §04.

The optical path

CX-6000/6010: 405 / 545 / 660 nm CX-9000: 405×2 / 545×4 / 660×3 / 800×1 nm

The analyzer auto-selects the most suitable wavelength for the assay type and the level of interference detected in the sample. CX-9000's extra channels and the 800 nm wavelength give it finer wavelength-switching resolution for HIL-affected samples.

HIL check — why it runs before every result

Hemolysis, Icterus, and Lipemia (H·I·L) are the two most common pre-analytical problems in coagulation testing. Before analysis, the analyzer runs a pre-analysis HIL bath and measures interference using multi-wavelength technology, then optimizes the analysis wavelength — switching to a secondary wavelength when H/I/L level is 1–2, or auto-diluting low/medium vs. high interference levels — before the primary test runs.

Sample Quality Validation (SQV) HIL pre-analysis check + primary sample volume check + sample clot check, run automatically on every sample before its result is reported — not an optional extra step the operator has to remember.
04 · Reading a result

The reaction curve

Every coagulation and immunoturbidimetric result is really a curve first, a number second. Reading the curve is how you tell a real clot from a bubble, a fibrin clump, or a probe artefact — do this before you tell a lab tech to trust or reject a flagged result.

The three landmark points

  • Start Point — marks where the reaction begins, after reagent and sample have mixed and the baseline optical reading stabilizes.
  • Clot Point (labelled 凝固点 / "Clotting point" in the analyzer software) — the time point of actual blood clotting. This is the number reported as the test result (PT, APTT, TT, FIB time).
  • End Point — marks where the reaction is complete and the signal has flattened into its final plateau.

A malformed curve — a double hump, a slope that never plateaus, a start point that drifts — is your first clue that a result should be re-examined before it's released, independent of whether it fell inside the reference range.

Response — how much signal there is to work with

Before any clotting-time algorithm runs, the analyzer computes how strong the reaction actually was between the start point and the end point. Two figures describe it, and both show up on every curve-detail screen:

FigureFormulaWhat it tells you
dOD — absorbance differencedOD = log₁₀(flux at start ÷ flux at end) × 1000Positively correlated with fibrinogen concentration.
dH — luminous flux changedH = flux at start − flux at endCharacterizes signal-to-noise ratio; directly affects clotting-time repeatability.

What the three curves actually are — and how they're computed

Everything above works from three curves, but only one of them is ever actually measured. The analyzer's optical sensor samples luminous flux at a fixed interval (Δt) throughout the reaction — that raw series of numbers is the original curve. The other two curves are not separately measured; they are computed from it, one differentiation step at a time:

Original curve

The raw signal: luminous flux (transmitted or scattered light) plotted against reaction time, sampled every Δt. As fibrin forms, the growing clot scatters light and transmitted flux falls — the curve's downward slope is the clot forming. Physiologically it tracks fibrinogen being converted into cross-linked fibrin polymer.

First derivative

Computed as the rate of change between consecutive samples: for each pair of adjacent points, ΔH (the flux change) divided by Δt (the fixed sampling interval). Plotting that slope value against time turns the original curve's steepest drop into a single peak — the peak marks the instant the signal was falling fastest. Physiologically, this tracks the rate of fibrin production (thrombin activity).

Second derivative

The same operation applied again, this time to the first-derivative curve: the rate of change of the rate of change. Because the first derivative peaks and then falls, its own derivative crosses zero at exactly that peak — turning a peak-finding problem into a zero-crossing problem. Physiologically, this tracks how fast fibrin generation is accelerating, related to prothrombinase (F Xa–F Va–Ca²⁺–PL) complex activity.

Because the second derivative is just "the derivative of the derivative," its zero-crossing and the first derivative's peak fall at the exact same instant — the reaction-curve diagram above shows why: the point where the original curve is steepest is the same point where its slope (first derivative) is at a maximum, which is the same point where the slope's own rate of change (second derivative) passes through zero. The two methods aren't measuring different events; they're two different arithmetic routes to the same clot point, chosen per test for whichever route holds up best against that test's typical curve shape (see "Why APTT uses the second derivative" below).

Why APTT uses the second derivative, not the first

  • Clinical fit — the second-derivative method is more sensitive for screening deficiencies in the intrinsic-pathway factors APTT exists to catch.
  • The direct reason: shoulder curves. APTT commonly produces a first-derivative peak with a flat top — a "shoulder" instead of a clean maximum. A flat top has no single unambiguous peak, so the first-derivative method's repeatability collapses on a meaningful share of APTT samples. The second derivative resolves the same transition as a zero-crossing, which stays sharp even when the first-derivative peak doesn't.
  • Response headroom — APTT's dOD runs roughly 2× PT's and 4× FIB's. That headroom absorbs the extra noise sensitivity a derivative calculation always carries.

Derivative vs. threshold — the tradeoff

Derivative methods (1st for PT, 2nd for APTT): only look at the derivative peak, so an odd-shaped curve elsewhere doesn't throw off the result, and clinical correlation is good (Kyowa Hospital found strong sensitivity/specificity against abnormal samples, e.g. hemophilia). Cost: SNR drops and repeatability worsens at low response — so these methods are reserved for high-response items that are rarely used as calibrators.

Threshold method (FIB): best repeatability of the three when response is low, and the lowest CV — good for calibration. Cost: easily thrown off by a curved tail or a biphasic waveform. Reserved for low-volume tests that rarely show an abnormal curve: FIB, individual coagulation factors, PS-a.

TT's fusion algorithm

Thrombin time covers an unusually wide range of clotting times, and no single method stays both sensitive and repeatable across all of it — so TT blends two methods, weighted by how long the reaction is taking:

TT's sensitivity to anticoagulant drugs is part of why it's kept as a screening test: against a Sysmex reference instrument, TT rose linearly with dabigatran concentration (R² ≈ 0.99) and with heparin concentration (R² ≈ 0.97) — good enough correlation on both to flag either drug's effect and track it over time.

Immunoturbidimetric & chromogenic curves — two-point and rate methods

D-Dimer, FDP, and AT III never show a derivative curve — only the original curve, which should fall in a smooth, monotonic, unbroken line as the reaction proceeds (no steps, no spikes). The clotting-time algorithms built for a Z-shaped clotting curve don't apply here; instead:

  • Two-point method (FDP, AT III) — the reported value comes from the absorbance difference between the start point and the end point, full stop.
  • D-Dimer fuses the two-point method with a rate method (the difference measured at the point of steepest slope) to smooth the transition on curves that aren't perfectly linear — the two algorithms hand off to keep the result continuous and accurate across the whole reportable range.

Reading a normal curve — the four-point check

Before trusting any result — flagged or not — run these four checks against the curve. This is the exact sequence to teach a new operator, and it's the backbone of the alarm-code library in §10.

  1. Curve shape. Clotting method: original curve is Z-shaped with a clear baseline/reaction/plateau; for derivative-calculated tests, the main peak (1st derivative for PT, 2nd derivative for APTT) is obvious and single. Immunoturbidimetric/chromogenic: original curve descends monotonically and smoothly — no steps, no spikes.
  2. Alarm information. If one fired, read the alarm code, prompt, possible cause, and operation suggestion together, and weigh them against the actual sample and test scenario before deciding the next step.
  3. Response (dH) check. Normal clotting-method response is generally dH > 1000. Materially lower, and you should confirm calculation accuracy against the curve and re-check sample/reagent condition before reporting.
  4. Luminous flux check. Normal baseline luminous flux for the clotting method is generally 10,000 – 50,000. A persistently low baseline points to sample interference — troubleshoot that before trusting the number.

Coagulation science backgrounder

The clinical grounding that lets you explain a flagged result to a doctor, not just to a lab tech. Hemostasis has two phases: primary (vasoconstriction, platelet adhesion and activation — the platelet-rich "white clot") and secondary (the coagulation cascade — the fibrin-rich "red clot," common in venous thrombosis).

Deep principle — what each test is actually doing

APTT — endogenous (intrinsic) pathway

Platelet-depleted plasma is incubated at 37 °C with the APTT reagent's phospholipids and contact activator. That combination activates the contact-phase intrinsic factors (F XII → F XI) without any tissue factor present — the reagent's whole job is to trigger the pathway APTT is named for while leaving the extrinsic pathway silent. Only after that incubation does CaCl₂ go in, recalcifying the plasma and starting the timed reaction through F IX/VIII → F X → common pathway → fibrin. The result reflects the combined activity of F XII, XI, IX, VIII plus the common-pathway factors X, V, II, I.

Clinical roles: screening the intrinsic + common pathways; catching a severe common-pathway deficiency; monitoring unfractionated heparin and other antithrombotic/direct thrombin inhibitor therapy; and — via the mixing test in §09 — testing for lupus anticoagulant.

PT — exogenous (extrinsic) pathway

One reagent, one step: the PT reagent already carries tissue factor, phospholipid, and calcium together, so it's added straight to citrated plasma with no separate activation or recalcification stage. Tissue factor drives F VII into the common pathway (F X → F V/II → fibrinogen → fibrin) directly — the extrinsic pathway is, by design, the short route. The result reflects F VII plus the same common-pathway factors APTT's back half reads (X, V, II, I).

PT is reported three different ways depending on what question is being asked — see the unit breakdown below.

FIB — fibrinogen concentration (Clauss principle)

The sample is first diluted 10:90 with IBS, which suppresses interference (including heparin effects) so the assay isolates fibrinogen's own behavior. The FIB reagent then adds excess thrombin — so much that every other factor's activity stops being rate-limiting, and clotting time becomes a function of fibrinogen concentration alone. Because the response is comparatively weak, FIB is one of the tests read by the threshold method (§04) rather than a derivative method, and the clotting time is converted to a concentration (g/L) against a calibration curve built from the FIB calibrator, not read directly in seconds.

Elevated and reduced FIB causes are in the table below.

TT — thrombin time, anticoagulant screen

The mirror image of FIB's design choice: TT reagent carries only a standard concentration of thrombin, added straight to undiluted plasma. Because thrombin isn't in excess here, TT is sensitive to anything that interferes with thrombin acting on fibrinogen — heparin (via antithrombin potentiation), direct thrombin inhibitors like dabigatran, abnormal fibrinogen molecules, and FDP/paraprotein interference. That sensitivity is the point: TT exists specifically to screen for an anticoagulant's presence, not to quantify fibrinogen.

Against a reference instrument, TT rose roughly linearly with both dabigatran and heparin concentration (R² ≈ 0.97 on both) — good enough correlation to flag either drug's effect and track it over time, even though TT alone can't distinguish which of the two is present.

A different principle you may see referenced: the mechanical (steel-ball) method Some coagulometers — and some generic reagent-kit literature — describe a mechanical detection principle instead of an optical one: a small steel ball is driven to move inside the reaction cuvette, a sensor tracks that movement, and the reported time is when the ball's movement stops or is damped by the forming clot. This is a real, established alternative used across the industry — but it is not how the CX series works. As established in §03, the CX-6000/6010/9000 all detect the clot optically, from scattered/transmitted light. Mention the mechanical method only as background if a customer's older instrument used it; don't describe it as how their CX analyzer works.

07 · What every day looks like

Daily operating workflow

This nine-step sequence is the backbone of §08's training curriculum — teach it as a loop the lab runs every single day, not a one-time install task.

Reagent handling — the old way vs. the CX way

Legacy bottle-based workflows on competing systems run six manual steps every time reagent is loaded: package → daily storage → preparation (30 min pre-warm) → reconstitution/mixing → manual loading → running out → repeat. Total: 15–30 minutes before testing, no online replacement.

CX-series cassette reagent collapses this to zero preparation — tear-and-use liquid reagent, RFID auto-read on loading, cross-seal against evaporation, ≥2-week onboard stability at 2–8 ℃. 09 · Factor deficiency vs. inhibitor

The APTT mixing test

A prolonged APTT with no obvious cause has two very different explanations — a coagulation factor deficiency (patient needs factor replacement) or a circulating inhibitor like lupus anticoagulant or a factor antibody (patient may need a completely different work-up path entirely). The mixing test is how a lab tells them apart, and CX-9000 is the one model in the lineup that runs the whole thing on-instrument. SQV is covered in §03; EQC is covered in §10's IQC/EQA section.

The theory: why mixing plasma 1:1 separates the two causes

  • Factor activity above ~50% reads as a normal APTT. When every endogenous coagulation factor's activity sits at 50% or above, APTT stays inside the reference range — different APTT reagents differ in exactly how sensitive they are to a given deficiency, which is why the reference range is verified per reagent rather than assumed.
  • A 1:1 mix pushes a simple deficiency back over that line. Patient Plasma (PP) lacking one or more factors, mixed 1:1 with Normal Pooled Plasma (NPP, effectively ~100% activity on every factor), lands the mixture's factor activity at roughly ≥50% — enough to correct APTT back into range. If that's what happens, the prolongation was a deficiency, not an inhibitor.
  • An inhibitor in PP suppresses the NPP's factors too. Heparin, a coagulation factor antibody, or lupus anticoagulant in the patient's plasma doesn't get diluted away from the mixture — it goes on suppressing factor activity in the added NPP as well, so APTT does not correct back to normal after mixing.

The seven specimens — what the analyzer actually runs, step by step

CX-9000 doesn't run one mixing test — it runs seven separate APTT measurements from the same PP/NPP pair, automatically, and derives the interpretation from how they compare. Every one of the seven follows the same underlying chemistry as a routine APTT (§03/§05): the plasma is warmed with APTT reagent — phospholipid + contact activator, acting as the mixed reagent — at 37 °C, then CaCl₂ is added as the trigger reagent to start the timed clot. 10 · When it goes wrong

Suggestions & troubleshooting

A study of 40,490 results from the Università degli Studi di Padova found errors cluster overwhelmingly in one place: most coagulation testing problems are pre-analytical, not instrument faults. Teach in this order — most of what a lab tech calls you about started before the sample ever reached the analyzer.

1 · Pre-analytical — collection & sample handling

Collection

  • Coagulation tube should be the second tube in the draw sequence (never first with a butterfly needle — draw a discard tube ahead of it).
  • Avoid drawing from the same side as an active infusion, and avoid drawing through a vascular access device (VAD) — both cause spurious dilution/heparin contamination.
  • Tourniquet time: 1 minute.
  • Mix 3–6 times by gentle inversion immediately after draw.
  • Anticoagulant: 3.2% sodium citrate, blood:anticoagulant ratio 9:1 — this ratio is critical; any deviation changes clotting time and invalidates the result.

Hematocrit correction formula

C = 1.85×10⁻³ × (100 − HCT) × Vblood

Apply when HCT > 55% (polycythemia) or < 20% (severe anemia) — adjust the citrate volume in the collection tube, or the blood:anticoagulant ratio silently drifts off 9:1 and clotting time reads falsely long.

Why it matters — and a worked example

The standard 9:1 blood:citrate ratio is calibrated around a roughly normal hematocrit. At an abnormal HCT, drawing the same fixed citrate volume no longer anticoagulates the same proportion of plasma — the citrate ends up concentrated relative to the (now smaller, at high HCT) plasma fraction. That excess citrate goes on to bind more of the CaCl₂ trigger reagent during testing than it should, which reads out as a falsely prolonged clotting time with no real coagulopathy behind it.

In short HCT compensation is a pre-analytical, manual step at blood collection — there is no on-instrument automatic HCT-compensation setting in the CX series' source documentation. It's a citrate-tube adjustment the phlebotomist/lab makes before the draw, not a correction the analyzer applies after the fact.
Typical case — short-tube adapter skipped
A hospital's C-2000A showed steadily worsening APTT/PT/INR across an EQA cycle (INR 0.96 → 7.71 over five runs). Root cause: the short-tube adapter wasn't used as required, throwing off the effective sample volume relative to anticoagulant.
Typical case — VAD draw
A hematology patient not on anticoagulants showed APTT >200 s and TT >100 s with a normal PT and FIB, QC in control, and no other abnormal samples that day. Cause: the sample was drawn through a vascular access device. Redraw normalized the result.

Centrifugation & specimen quality

  • 1500 g for 15 minutes → platelet-poor plasma (platelets < 10×10⁹/L). Under-centrifuged plasma stays rich in phospholipids/platelets and skews PT/APTT.
  • Refuse to test a hemolyzed sample — released hemoglobin and phospholipid make the result unreliable, not just noisy.
  • Lipemic samples: the mechanical/optical method used here is not affected by icterus, lipemia, or hemolysis as directly as some older systems — but for D-Dimer specifically, dilution reduces interference when lipemia is present.

Patient status & interfering medications

Keep the patient fasting and at rest before draw. Vitamin K and vitamin K antagonists, heparin, and direct oral anticoagulants (dabigatran, rivaroxaban) all shift results independent of any analyzer issue — always ask about diet and medication before troubleshooting the machine.

2 · Analytical — reagent & instrument status

Typical case — reagent cross-contamination
A batch of D-Dimer results came back all-positive, no negatives, right after a DD buffer replacement. Root cause: the new DD buffer had been mixed with FIB reagent, which carries thrombin — thrombin cleaves soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin, which the optics read as a light change and reported as an elevated DD result. Fixed by replacing the reagent correctly, unmixed.
  • Don't keep a reagent lid open longer than necessary — contamination and evaporation both distort results.
  • Never top up an opened reagent position with a new lot mixed into the old — treat each lot as its own inventory.
  • PT reagent batch changed → reset the ISI (International Sensitivity Index); if different PT reagent batches carry different ISI values, the difference must be corrected before results are comparable.
  • DD / FDP / FIB / AT-III batch changed → recalibrate. These are lot-sensitive in a way PT's ISI correction doesn't cover.

D-Dimer / FDP — reading contradictory results

  • D-Dimer's half-life is 6–8 h; a result that swings from very high to normal within 24 h can be real biology (fibrin formation stopped), not an error.
  • If DD and FDP disagree (DD is normally ≥ FDP): a low value below cutoff has no clinical significance and can be reported as-is; a high value above cutoff needs a serial dilution (1:8, 1:16…) — if it dilutes linearly, it's a true positive; if not, suspect a false positive (interference) and consider cross-checking on a different detection system.
  • High D-Dimer/FDP is expected and not necessarily pathological in pregnancy — pregnant patients run hypercoagulable with 2–3× normal fibrinogen, so use clinical context, not the non-pregnant reference range, when interpreting.

Four worked cases, from real curves

Case 1 — Slight coagulation, low fibrinogen (48301000)

PT flagged slightly coagulated. Original curve is still recognizably Z-shaped and the first-derivative peak is still measurable — but dH is only 779, well under the >1000 normal-response bar from §04. A repeat FIB came back at 0.6 g/L, confirming genuinely low fibrinogen rather than an instrument problem. Reported as *13.8 s.

Lesson: a low-but-present dH with a real clinical explanation on recheck is a reportable result, not an automatic rejection.

Case 2 — "No coagulation detected," but the timeout needs judgment (48361000)

TT reported >150 s, "no coagulation reaction detected." The patient was on heparin with a normal FIB — a genuinely prolonged TT was clinically expected, not an error. Auto re-exam extended the run into delayed mode (360 s) and reported *265.12 s. Because the curve still hadn't reached a plateau at 360 s, the true value may run even higher — the right move is to request a further-extended Measure Time (e.g. 480 s) rather than treat *265.12 s as final.

Lesson: this is exactly the "low-but-nonzero response" scenario in the caution box above — a true timeout has no response, this sample had a visibly moving curve.

Case 3 — Biphasic waveform

A curve that drops in two distinct stages instead of one clean transition. In APTT the literature attributes the early, false drop to precipitation of Ca²⁺-dependent VLDL/CRP complexes — itself of research interest for sepsis and early DIC, but not the true clot. Rule either way: the first drop is not the real reaction — the second drop is the true clotting curve. In this PT example (cause unclear, as it often is for PT biphasics) the first derivative peak at roughly 8 s is the false signal; the true, later peak is what gets reported.

Lesson: a "derivative multi-peak" alarm (48307000) is your cue to check which peak is which before accepting either.

Case 4 — HIL interference (lipemia)

PT flagged *15.87 s with "interference too strong" (48309000-series). Curve check: baseline luminous flux had collapsed to roughly 110 — against the normal 10,000–50,000 range from §04 — and the first-derivative peak was barely distinguishable from noise. dOD was still usable even though dH and the derivative peak weren't clean, so the starred result was reportable as-is, but a repeat after high-speed centrifugation to clear the lipemia gives a cleaner answer.

Lesson: collapsed baseline flux is the tell — go straight to the HIL result on that sample rather than troubleshooting the analyzer.

13 · Keeping it in good working order

System maintenance

Two tracks, covered end to end below: scheduled maintenance the analyzer runs itself, and five demand-based programs the operator runs from Semi-Auto Perform when a specific trigger shows up — plus covers, logs, temperature/channel checks, wearing parts, reagents and cuvettes. Sourced from IFU Chapter 11.

13.1 · Scheduling automatic daily maintenance

  1. Open the setup screen. On the analyzer software, click System → Daily Maint. → Maintenance Setup.
  2. Set the idle windows. Click Auto Maintenance Setup and set up to two time periods when the lab is relatively idle.
  3. Confirm. Click OK.
How the two windows are used If the analyzer is idle during a specified window, daily maintenance starts automatically. If it's still working through that window, the analyzer waits and starts daily maintenance in the next configured window instead — it never interrupts a run in progress.

When the analyzer won't be used for more than a week — prevents cleaning-solution crystallization in the lines.

Soak and Rinse

Run weekly.

Before starting: Cleaning Solution and waste containers emptied and connected; probe cleanser in the reagent disk sufficient; analyzer in standby.

  1. Set the analyzer to standby. Click System → Daily Maint. → Semi-Auto Perform, select Soak and Rinse, click Execute.
  2. Click the components to be rinsed, and set the Soak duration.
  3. Click Start.

  2. Set the Execution times, and click Start.

Before restarting analysis after > 1 week idle.

  2. Set the Prime times, and click Start.

Reviewing & printing maintenance history

  2. Click Filter to set modules, maintenance types, items and time range, then OK — or click the print icon at the bottom of the screen to print the log directly.

13.3 · Cleaning the analyzer covers

Use a clean cotton cloth. Perform only when necessary, per the site's own hospital maintenance plan — repeated disinfection can degrade the covers over time.

Recommended Water, 70% isopropyl alcohol.
Prohibited 75% ethanol, 3% hydrogen peroxide, or any material that may corrode metal.
Biohazard reminder If samples or reagents drop on a cover, clean and disinfect it. Wear gloves, lab coat and glasses, and follow the lab's safe-handling procedures for the contacted areas. Mindray does not claim these agents' validity for infection control — for effective infection-control guidance, consult the hospital's Infection Prevention Department.

13.6 · Reagent management

The "Reagent" screen gives real-time status and replacement for every loaded reagent, plus cuvettes and Cleaning Solution — four areas: the reagent area (what's loaded), the reagent volume area (remaining tests; turns red when insufficient), the consumable volume area (cuvette feeder, cuvette trash, Cleaning Solution), and the function buttons (Rotate the disk, Quota Management, Details on a selected reagent — position, name, lot number, expiry date, open-vial date, remaining volume).

Each reagent card shows its name, remaining volume/specification (as number of tests supported), and opened days — displayed in red once a reagent exceeds its open-vial validity (cleaning/diluent reagents show uncapping time in hours instead, also red once past normal time). An expired reagent's card displays "Expired" outright.

Handling reagents safely Reagents irritate eyes, skin and mucous membranes — wear gloves, lab coat and glasses. If reagent contacts skin or eyes, rinse with ample clean water and seek medical attention immediately. Use only Mindray-specified reagents; check every package for leakage or moisture damage before use, and never mix a new reagent with residue left in the container being replaced. After installing a new container, let it sit briefly before use. When changing to a new lot of FIB/c, FDP/c, D-D/c, or AT III/Activity reagent, recalibrate to establish a new calibration curve.

Disposing expired reagent

  1. Open the reagent disk cover, take out the expired reagent, and dispose of it per the lab's own procedures.

Loading new reagents

  1. Prepare the reagent per its own instructions for use.
  2. Open the reagent disk.
  3. Tear the film on the reagent kit, place it into the disk through the inlet, and press it to the bottom.
  4. Close the reagent disk cover — the "Reagent" screen refreshes automatically.

Up to 3 dual-reagent kits can be loaded at once; press the rotate button to bring more positions to the inlet. The disk holds up to 15 dual-reagent kits — 30 reagent positions in total.

13.7 · Cuvettes & waste

Adding cuvettes

The feeder holds up to 500 cuvettes; a low-cuvette alarm lights the consumables panel orange. A full cuvette bag also holds 500 — pour in what's needed.

  1. Open the cuvette feeder and pour the cuvettes in directly.
  2. Place the RFID card from the cuvette bag onto the RFID scan area on the main unit — the analyzer reads the quantity and refreshes the compartment status automatically.

Don't discard the reagent box before scanning its RFID card, or the loaded quantity won't be charged to the software and tests may not proceed.

Clearing the cuvette trash box

  1. Open the cabinet door (CX-6000) and pull out the trash box.
  2. Dispose of the cuvettes per lab protocol, removing the plastic bag that holds them.
  3. Push the box back in with a fresh plastic bag.
  4. On the software, click Reagent → Clear Waste, select Cvt. Trash Box, and click Reset.

Replacing the waste container

Biohazard Samples, reagents, controls, calibrators and waste are potentially infectious — wear PPE and follow safe-handling procedures. Never replace the waste container while the analyzer is working, to avoid waste spraying out; follow the on-instrument prompt.
  1. Remove the cap of a new container and set it beside the one being replaced.
  2. Transfer the waste into the new container.
  3. Re-cap the (now full) waste container for disposal.

Replacing the Cleaning Solution container

Check daily, before startup, that Cleaning Solution is sufficient — a low-level alarm lights the consumables panel orange during the day.

  1. Open the cabinet below the analyzer (CX-6000).
  2. Open the cap of the new container with the uncapping device, and set it beside the old one.
  3. Remove the supporting board under the old cap, then unscrew the old cap counterclockwise and lift out the cap assembly carefully.
  4. Insert the pickup tube into the new container and turn the cap clockwise until secure.
  5. Install the supporting board under the new container's cap.

Afterward, confirm the tube connected to the cap assembly isn't bent, and that the supporting board sits under the new cap.

13.8 · Preparing "Clean Pro" (custom probe-cleanser kit)

Mindray's standard blood-coagulation probe cleanser contains substances that don't meet China's export standards, so it can't be shipped overseas pre-mixed. Outside China, the kit has to be prepared and loaded on site instead — this is that recipe, the first-load software setup, and the replacement schedule. Source: Mindray "Probe cleanser configuration" service note, 2024.12.03.

Not the same product as "Clean Solution" §13.6/§13.7 above cover Clean Solution (REF 105-023651-00) — the fluidics Cleaning Solution kept connected to the analyzer at all times. Clean Pro is different: a custom-prepared probe-cleanser kit, mixed on site and loaded onto the reagent disk like any other reagent. Don't confuse the two containers.

General configuration (small batch)

  1. Combine. 1.5 mL Probe Cleanser (PROBE CLEANSER M-68/EN, 50 mL×1, REF 105-002225-A0) + 9.5 mL Cleanser (REF 105-015487-00) + 10 mL 10% sodium chloride solution.
  2. Mix. Tighten the cap and invert 10 times.

No medical 10% NaCl on hand?

Prepare it manually (e.g. 100 mL batch) either of two ways:

  • Weigh 10.69 g NaCl (CAS 7647-14-5), add 96.19 g pure water, stir until clear with no solids.
  • Or weigh 10 g NaCl, dissolve in ~80 mL pure water, then top up to 100 mL at 25 ℃.

Any 10% (w/v) NaCl-and-water-only solution works — medical 10% NaCl injection included.

Preparing a bulk batch

Scaling the same ratio up (e.g. to 1 L at once) is fine — store the batch at 2–8 ℃, and use it within 60 days.

First-load setup in software

Only needed the first time an adapter position is loaded with Clean Pro — usually done by the install engineer. After that, a freshly prepared kit loads with no extra software steps.

CX-9000
  1. On the loading-disk screen, click the target position, then Edit Info.
  2. Set Reagent Name → Clean Pro and Assay Kit Type → PC Kit; leave everything else blank. Click OK.
  3. Open the loading-disk cover, place the kit in that position, and close the cover — the analyzer detects the volume automatically.
CX-6000 / CX-6010
  1. Open the loading-disk cover, place the kit in the target position, and close the cover.
  2. Click that position, then Edit Info.
  3. Set Reagent Name → Clean Pro and Assay Kit Type → PC Kit; leave everything else blank. Click OK.
  4. Click Reagent Change — the analyzer detects the volume automatically.

Replacing the kit — every 30 days

Contaminants accumulate in the kit over time, with a growing risk of cross-contamination — it isn't meant to run indefinitely. After 30 days in use, the software raises: "Please replace the X position CleanPro kit, otherwise the test accuracy will be affected." Prepare and load a fresh kit, then click Eliminate to clear the prompt.
