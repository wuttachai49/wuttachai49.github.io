01

Sepsis & the case for blood culture

Why the instrument exists: blood culture is the diagnostic anchor for bloodstream infection and sepsis, and the speed and accuracy of the FA-N system feed directly into antibiotic decisions.

Sepsis 3.0 (2016, 31 international societies) defines sepsis as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection — operationally, infection + a rise in SOFA score of ≥2. In-hospital mortality exceeds 10% for sepsis and 40% for septic shock. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign bundle time fell from 140 minutes (2014) to 64 minutes (2017) as hospitals streamlined the pathway — blood culture before antibiotics is one of the five bundle elements, alongside lactate measurement, broad-spectrum antimicrobials, and IV fluids.

Why the culture result matters clinically

A positive blood culture lets clinicians move from empirical to targeted therapy: identify the causative organism, confirm the source of infection, and run susceptibility testing. Getting this right is estimated to reduce infection-related mortality by 20–30%, shorten length of stay, and reduce the emergence of antimicrobial resistance. The CDC estimates over 50% of antibiotic use in emergency departments is inappropriate or unnecessary absent culture data — the instrument's job is to close that gap as fast as biology allows.

Note

Specimen hierarchy: sterile body fluids (blood, CSF, pleural/ascitic fluid) are the most clinically valuable specimen class in microbiology, and blood culture is the primary automated culture method for this class — unlike urine or respiratory specimens, a positive result here is rarely dismissed as colonization.

Standardized collection & submission

Most "instrument problems" reported from the ward are actually pre-analytical. This is the checklist to walk a nurse or phlebotomy team through.

When to draw

Timing, sets, and volume

  • Before antibiotics — collect as soon as BSI is suspected, before the first (or next) dose.
  • 2–3 sets from different sites within 24h for adults; don't repeat for 2–5 days. Endocarditis: 3 sets up front, +2 more if negative at 24h (5 total).
  • Adult bottle: 5–10 mL (max 10 mL) — closer to 10 mL improves recovery. Peds bottle: 1–3 mL (max 3 mL). Blood-to-broth ratio target 1:5–1:10 to dilute complement, antibodies, phagocytes and lysozyme that inhibit growth.
  • Two sites (not just two bottles from one draw) — single-side draws miss 15–17% of pathogens, and paired aerobic+anaerobic bottles recover ~15% more staphylococci, Enterobacterales and anaerobes than aerobic-only.
  • Pediatric patients: volume by body weight; anaerobic culture is not routine in children.
Critical

Inoculation order matters — getting it backwards is a leading cause of false-negative anaerobic cultures:

  • Direct venipuncture (butterfly/straight needle): aerobic bottle first, then anaerobic.
  • Syringe draw: anaerobic bottle first, then aerobic — a syringe carries residual air that would purge the anaerobic bottle's atmosphere if inoculated second.

Technique

  1. Remove the vial's plastic cap; disinfect the rubber stopper with 70% isopropyl alcohol (or 75% ethanol) and let it air-dry. Barcode/label area on the vial is marked off-limits — don't cover the fill line or scan zone.
  2. Disinfect the venipuncture site with chlorhexidine or 2% iodine tincture (age >2 months); use 70% isopropanol instead of chlorhexidine for infants <2 months.
  3. Inoculate in the correct order (above); shake gently to mix, avoid clot formation.
  4. Label immediately: patient name, unique identifier, date/time of collection, collector identity.
  5. Transport to the lab within 2 hours of collection — do not refrigerate or heat the bottle.

04

Detection principle & algorithms

Everything downstream — TTD, false positives, the slow-growth case — comes back to one signal: reflectance at the bottom of the bottle, read every 10 minutes.

Colorimetry: the physical signal

Every MR PLUS bottle has a CO₂-sensitive colorimetric sensor bonded into its base. As microorganisms metabolize the broth, they produce CO₂, which shifts the sensor's pH and its color. An LED/photodetector pair under every cell reads the sensor every 10 minutes; as the film lightens, more light passes through and reflectance rises. The algorithm layer only has to decide when a rising reflectance curve means "positive." This is a fully noninvasive optical read — nothing punctures or samples the bottle.

Which algorithm fires depends on where the culture already is in its growth curve when loaded, not on a fixed clock. The two scenarios below are handled by entirely different method families, so they're shown as two separate timelines rather than one shared clock.

The three algorithm families, method by method

FA-N doesn't run one detection rule — it runs three families of methods, and picks between them based on where in the bacterial growth curve (lag → log → stationary) the culture is sitting when the signal is evaluated. All of them work on the same raw input — the reflectance curve sampled every 10 minutes — but differ in whether they judge an absolute level, a slope, or a change in slope (acceleration). The more curve shape available, the earlier and more confidently a method can call positive.

Early algorithms — for delayed bottles (missed lag phase)

Applied when a bottle already contains an actively-dividing population at load time (long pre-analytical delay, poor cold-chain, etc.) — there's no clean lag phase to anchor a slope calculation against, so the three methods progressively substitute a coarser signal for a finer one:

Early Rate slope (1st derivative) Reads the rate of change of reflectance in the initial culture window — the most information-dense signal, so it fires fastest. Used when enough consecutive points already show a consistent upward slope. 1h 00m
Early Acceleration 2nd derivative (slope-of-slope) The bottle shows only a short lag phase before instrument loading; the method looks for an accelerating (steepening) trend in the early slope rather than requiring a stable rate — catches bottles the Early Rate method would still be waiting on. 1h 20m

Each method, in full

Every method below is timed from the bottle's own first loading into the instrument — not from lag-phase onset, not from a fixed daily schedule. That reference point is what makes "1h 20m" a meaningful, comparable number across bottles that were loaded at completely different real-world times.

Early Rate early · 1h 00m

"For delayed bottles (missed lag phase), positivity is reported by detecting the slope change in the initial stage of culture." The fastest-firing method in the whole system — it needs the least curve shape, just a clear enough rate-of-change signal.

Early Acceleration early · 1h 20m

"Delayed bottles [that have] a short lag phase in the process of instrument culture" are reported "by detecting the slope acceleration trend in the initial stage of culture." Catches bottles whose rate signal isn’t stable enough yet for Early Rate, but whose curve is visibly steepening.

Threshold (early) early · 1h 40m

"Delayed bottles would be reported positive by detecting the absolute reflectance of the culture bottle at the initial stage. When the absolute reflectance of the initial period reached a certain threshold, the positivity result was reported." No slope needed at all — the fallback when a curve shape isn’t usable yet.

Acceleration (normal) normal · 3h 00m

"For bottles that are timely loaded, positivity is reported by detecting an accelerating trend in slope growth." The earliest call available to a properly-timed bottle that started in true lag phase.

Rate (normal) normal · 3h 40m

"For bottles that are timely loaded, positive results can be reported by detecting the slope growth trend." More conservative than Acceleration — used when the accelerating-trend signal alone isn’t yet decisive.

Slow-growth unique · >2 days

For organisms whose "growth curve is relatively flat," where "early and normal algorithms may not be able to detect them" at all. See the full mechanism below.

Slow-growth algorithm — unique to FA-N

Fastidious or slow/weak-growing organisms (classic case: Penicillium marneffei; also relevant for Candida glabrata, Cryptococcus neoformans) produce a curve that is relatively flat — too flat for early or normal methods to ever trigger, so the bottle would otherwise sit at "negative-to-date" until it times out.

  • Trigger condition: once a bottle has been negative-to-date for more than ~2 days, the instrument begins accumulating CO₂ signal data specifically to look for this pattern.
  • Mechanism: red blood cells in the bottle also respire and slowly produce their own background CO₂ signal. The algorithm builds a simulated model of that blood-cell respiration curve from the bottle's own historical reflectance data, then subtracts it from the measured curve — what's left over is a recovered bacterial/fungal gas-production signal that would otherwise be buried in cellular noise.
  • Result: a documented case of Penicillium marneffei that TDR-X reported negative was correctly flagged positive by FA-N at 73.8 hours (3.07 days) — inside the instrument's incubation window, and well ahead of the ~6.6 ± 3.0 day average time-to-diagnosis reported in the literature for blood-culture-based diagnosis of this organism.
Why it matters

This is the one algorithm family that's a genuine architectural difference from the previous-generation TDR-X platform, not just a tuning improvement — it's the reason to specifically ask a customer running TDR-X about unexplained "negative-to-date" fungal or fastidious-organism cases when positioning an upgrade to FA-N.

05

Incubation & temperature management

This is the section to reach for whenever a site reports an unexplained cluster of false positives — more often than not it traces back to temperature, not biology.

Solid (metal) direct heating

Each incubation module is heated independently by direct contact with a metal block rather than by heating the surrounding air. Rise time to target is under 30 minutes, and steady-state fluctuation stays ≤0.3℃ — the ambient room only needs to sit 3℃ below the setpoint. Because heat is stored in metal mass rather than air, opening a chamber door barely disturbs it: air-heated designs show a large temperature swing on door-open, direct-heated designs show a small one.

Mechanism

How a false positive from temperature actually happens: a temperature rise reduces CO₂ solubility in the medium → local pH increases → the sensor film darkens → reflectance drops sharply → the instrument reads the fluctuating rising edge of that signal as bacterial growth. This is why sites that cut the air-conditioning at night, or sit a unit against an external wall, see false positives cluster overnight.

Site conditions that prevent this

  • Keep room temperature stable — don't schedule the AC to shut off overnight in the lab housing the instrument.
  • Leave ≥20 cm clearance behind the unit; keep it away from heat sources, direct sun, and vents.
  • The instrument's own fan enhances heat dissipation to reduce false positives from excess ambient heat, but it cannot compensate for a room that swings by several degrees.
  • FA-N applies an automatic temperature-compensation function that corrects curve fluctuations caused by ambient swings before they reach the algorithm layer — this is the main reason FA-N's false-positive rate (≤1%) improves on the earlier TDR-X platform in the same room conditions.

For troubleshooting a suspected temperature-driven false positive, export the incubation temperature log by day or hour (System → Maintenance → Export Temperature) and overlay it against the bottle's flagged timestamp.

06

Daily operation

The instrument runs continuously, 24 hours a day — only power it down when necessary.

Loading bottles

The workflow differs by model because only FA-N120/FA-N240 carry the bottle information collection module (barcode + blood-volume camera).

FA-N60 — manual / external scanner

  1. Scan the bottle barcode with the external scanner, or open System → Sample → Load to open the Load Bottles dialog and enter/scan information manually.
  2. Select sample type if prompted.
  3. Insert the bottle bottom-first into any white, steadily-lit (free) cell — one bottle at a time.
  4. The cell indicator turns off once the bottle is fully seated. Close the incubator.

FA-N120 / FA-N240 — auto-collect

  1. Hold the bottle by the neck and place it gently on the bottle information collection position, aligning the fill-line triangle to the "Detection Area" silkscreen triangle.
  2. Wait for the prompt tone — barcode and blood volume are captured automatically. Rotate the bottle slightly if it isn't recognized.
  3. Select sample type manually only if the system doesn't infer it.
  4. Insert into any free (white) cell; close the incubator.
Tip

If blood volume or barcode isn't recognized on first placement, rotate the bottle slightly rather than re-placing it — the collection module reads from two internal side-scanners plus the main camera, and a small rotation usually resolves it.

Barcode rules, in depth

Sample and patient barcodes vary by institution, so the instrument's barcode reading is rule-driven, not hard-coded. This is one of the highest-value things an application specialist can get right at go-live, since a wrong rule silently produces "unreadable barcode" prompts on every single bottle.

  • Under Setup → Barcode Systems → Barcode rules, an administrator writes a regular expression for the sample barcode and, separately, for the patient barcode — each can independently be toggled "required."
  • A rule-check field lets you paste a real barcode sample and validate the regex live, before it goes into production — do this with an actual bottle/label from the site, not a hypothetical one.
  • On FA-N120/FA-N240, a second tab (Built-in scanner code systems) lets an administrator enable/disable specific barcode symbologies and control whether the scanner's own check-digit character is validated and/or forwarded — changes here require an instrument restart to take effect.

Caution

Make the sample-barcode rule and patient-barcode rule mutually exclusive whenever the site uses two distinct label formats — if a single barcode instance can match both regexes, the software cannot tell which kind it just scanned, and the rule-check step will flag it as ambiguous before you ever leave the install visit.

Maximum Test Time (Max. TTD), in depth

  • Range is 1–42 days, factory default 5 days for all bottle types — never cut this arbitrarily; a shortened window converts a genuinely slow-growing organism into a false negative.
  • Can be set two ways: by bottle type (Setup → Maximum TTD — applies to bottles loaded after the change) or per individual bottle (on the Load Bottles screen when loading, or via Edit Bottle/Result Details afterward for a bottle already in progress).
  • Changing the type-level default has no retroactive effect — bottles already loaded keep the Max. TTD they were assigned at load time.
  • This is the same value referenced by Setup → Primary Negative Results reporting and by the slow-growth algorithm's ~2-day trigger window (§04) — a Max. TTD set too short can end a test before the slow-growth mechanism gets a chance to run.

Composition

Each vial combines a peptone/bio-extract nitrogen source (≥2.2–2.4% w/v), glucose (≥0.05% w/v), sodium polyanethol sulfonate (SPS, an anticoagulant that also inhibits complement and phagocytes — ≥0.05% w/v), amino acids and vitamins, brain–heart infusion or tryptic soy broth as basal medium, plus a headspace gas mix of N₂/O₂(/CO₂ for anaerobic). Two additions are specific to the MR PLUS line:

Fastidious factors

Offset the nutrients a resin-heavy medium would otherwise strip out, promoting growth of Streptococcus pyogenes, S. pneumoniae and similar fastidious organisms even without added blood.

Anti-adsorption factors

Inhibit fungi such as Candida glabrata and Cryptococcus neoformans from being physically trapped/killed on the resin surface itself — a known failure mode in resin-heavy bottles.

Antimicrobial adsorption — why it exists

A patient started on antibiotics before the blood draw carries residual drug into the bottle; without neutralization, that drug keeps suppressing the pathogen inside the bottle, prolonging TTP or causing an outright false negative. MR PLUS resin comes in two types:

  • Macroporous adsorbent resin — physical adsorption via van der Waals forces (homogeneous → membrane → particle diffusion) of non-polar antimicrobials; capacity depends on pore-size distribution, surface area and particle load.
  • Cationic resin — negatively charged, adsorbs basic drugs (mainly aminoglycosides) by ion exchange.

MR's resin upgrade over the prior generation stacks three changes: pore-size ratio optimized for a broader range of antimicrobial molecular weights, specific surface area +100%, and particle content 3× — together roughly 3× the neutralization capacity, engineered to a balance point (too little resin under-adsorbs; too much can itself suppress fastidious growth).

Middleware & satellite blood culture

The Microbe Information Management Software links multiple FA-N units and the LIS — its real job is eliminating the single biggest source of delay in the whole pathway: the walk from ward to lab.

TAT driver

Pre-analytical transport delay is consistently the largest controllable component of overall TTP. A satellite instrument at the point of collection can cut this to near zero — the tradeoff is always staffing, not technology.

Three satellite deployment models

① In the clinical department ICU / ED

Best TAT. Completely removes machine-related delay; improves positive rate and shortens TTP.

Trade-off: hard to staff — clinical departments rarely want ownership of blood-culture handling, and there's no direct clinical incentive for the ward to run it.

② At the lab's own specimen desk recommended default

Best staffing fit. Same TAT/positive-rate benefit; night-shift lab staff already own it.

Trade-off: still exposed to delay between collection and delivery to the specimen desk itself.

③ At a branch hospital network model

Positive bottles are transferred to the central lab for subculture/ID/AST; middleware provides cross-campus data connection, transmission, viewing and audit.

Fits a hub-and-spoke hospital network — the model most relevant across S.E. Supply's own multi-hospital footprint.

Free-sign & pre-connection — removing manual accessioning

  • Free sign: the instrument links bottle information and patient information automatically on load; the LIS auto-generates the lab sample number, so no separate manual "receiving" step is needed.
  • LIS pre-connection: sample ID, patient ID and bottle ID are bound in the LIS at the point of bottle labeling — the instrument then only needs to recognize any one of the three identifiers, and the rest link automatically.

QA / QC & performance verification

Verification is a one-time event at implementation, distinct from routine QC, which is ongoing. Knowing which one a customer is asking for changes what you hand them.

Definition

Performance verification confirms a new, unmodified, regulatory-cleared test performs to the manufacturer's stated specifications (accuracy, precision, measuring interval) — done once, at new implementation. It is not the same exercise as routine quality control, and is not repeated on a schedule unless something material changes (new lot family, relocation, major repair).

Mindray-recommended verification protocol

  1. Confirm readiness — instrument installed and operating normally; all consumables/materials on hand; confirm verification is required under local/lab regulations.
  2. Agree the protocol with the lab in writing before starting.
  3. Assemble materials: instrument (FA-N or TDR-X), TDR/MR resin bottles (aerobic/peds/anaerobic), recommended culture media per organism group (nutrient/blood/MacConkey agar for Enterobacteriaceae and non-fermenters; blood agar for staph; chocolate + blood agar for strep and fastidious organisms; Sabouraud's/corn agar for yeasts), plus sterile swabs, 75% alcohol, syringes, saline, sterile tubes and defibrillated/fresh sheep blood.
  4. Select strains — ATCC, QC, or other known strains; at least 5 strains per bottle type, covering the categories below.

Inoculum preparation (bacteria)

  1. Subculture stock strains to nonselective media twice before testing.
  2. Prepare a 0.5 McFarland suspension (~10⁸ CFU/mL).
  3. Serial-dilute: 0.10 mL into 9.90 mL sterile saline three times in sequence → 10⁶ → 10⁴ → 100 CFU/mL.
  4. Wipe the puncture site with 75% alcohol; inoculate 0.3 mL of the 100 CFU/mL suspension into the bottle (add 10 mL / 3 mL sterile defibrillated sheep blood to aerobic/peds bottles when the target organism is fastidious, e.g. H. influenzae).
  5. Load into the instrument; incubate 5 days or until a positive is reported.
  6. In parallel, plate 0.3 mL of the same 100 CFU/mL suspension for colony counting after 24h — target actual inoculum 5–30 CFU/bottle (theoretical 30 CFU/bottle). Gram stain and identify every positive.

Yeast preparation follows the same logic starting from a 0.5 McFarland fungal suspension (~10⁶ CFU/mL), diluted in two steps to 100 CFU/mL.

Reading the colony count

If a bottle fails to signal positive within 5 days and the confirmatory colony count is below the LOD, that points to a strain-activity or workflow problem (poor viability, dilution error, contamination during prep) — not necessarily an instrument fault. Always plate-count before concluding the instrument failed verification.

Pass / fail criteria

Criteria

Pass: ≥80% (at least 4 of 5 strains per bottle type) detected within the manufacturer's specified time (5 days).
Fail: ≥2 of 5 strains undetected in time — investigate root cause and re-verify. A device that fails verification must not be used for clinical testing until it passes.

False results — the troubleshooting core

This is the section a site call almost always maps back to. Work it as a decision tree: is the discordant signal on the positive side or the negative side, and is it instrument, bottle, sample, or technique?

Standardized reporting — the baseline before troubleshooting a "wrong" result

  • A record should exist in the LIS the moment a blood culture is ordered, updated at every status change.
  • Reporting "positive for growth" without an accompanying Gram stain is never acceptable.
  • Critical-risk result: issued for every positive blood culture, and for any change to a previously issued result — must reach the clinician within 60 minutes of laboratory verification.
  • An unacceptable specimen (broken bottle, unlabeled/mislabeled) is itself reportable as a critical-risk event, not silently discarded.

False positive — definition & causes

CLSI M47: an instrument signal indicating growth for which subcultures and stains are negative, or a recovered isolate later judged not to be the cause of the bloodstream infection.

① Contamination

Contamination rates run 1–10%+ across labs; ≤3% is the accepted ceiling. The principal source is commensal skin flora introduced during collection — indwelling vascular catheters are a second recognized source.

Usually a contaminantRarely a contaminant
Cutibacterium (formerly Propionibacterium) acnes, Micrococcus, Bacillus spp. (excl. B. anthracis), coagulase-negative staphylococci (excl. S. lugdunensis), Aerococcus, Corynebacterium spp. (excl. C. jeikeium)S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, β-hemolytic streptococci, Listeria monocytogenes, E. coli & other Enterobacterales, P. aeruginosa, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae, anaerobic Gram-negative rods, Candida spp.

Prevention checklist: appropriate patient selection (don't culture very-low-pretest-probability patients) → alcohol-based skin antisepsis with full drying time → 70% isopropyl alcohol for the bottle stopper (iodine alone is insufficient) → prefer venipuncture over indwelling catheters → correct blood volume, neither under- nor over-filled → two separate collection sites → phlebotomy team training → ongoing contamination-rate surveillance with feedback to collectors.

② Environmental / instrument factors

Incubation temperature fluctuation is the headline mechanism (see §5) — a room whose AC cycles off overnight, or a unit installed with insufficient clearance, produces temperature-driven signal fluctuations that read as a rising positivity curve. Also check for voltage instability, module communication faults, and general instrument electrical noise.

③ Sample or consumable factors

  • Overfilled bottle — excess blood cells respire and produce their own CO₂, mimicking bacterial signal.
  • Acidic blood pH — can independently shift the sensor's color.
  • Very high WBC count (malignancy, hematologic disease patients) — elevated background respiration.
  • Expired or improperly stored bottles, or bottles refrigerated after collection — never refrigerate or heat a culture bottle.

④ Organism-related false positives (positive signal, but organism not visible on Gram stain)

  • Atypically shaped/sized organisms — e.g. Abiotrophia spp. can appear bizarre; Francisella spp. can be so small they blend into slide background.
  • Poorly Gram-stain-visualized organisms (e.g. Mycoplasma spp.) — an acridine orange stain may be needed, or Gram stain with a carbol fuchsin counterstain (useful for Campylobacter, Helicobacter, Brucella).

⑤ Culture-condition mismatches

  • Abiotrophia defectiva / Granulicatella won't grow on plain blood agar.
  • Small curved Gram-negative rods (e.g. Campylobacter-like organisms) need specialized medium, incubated at both 35–37℃ and 42℃ for ≥72h, microaerobic.
  • Francisella tularensis is easily missed on Gram stain and may grow only on chocolate agar.
  • HACEK group may need supplemented media and/or 5–10% CO₂; colonies can take 48h post-subculture to appear.
  • Others requiring special handling: Helicobacter, Legionella, Leptospira, Mycoplasma.

False negative — definition & causes

CLSI M47: a confirmed bloodstream infection with negative cultures, or growth confirmed on subculture/stain that the instrument never flagged.

On arrival, ask

  • What changed right before this started? (staff, room AC, new lot of bottles, power event)
  • Positive-side or negative-side complaint?
  • One bottle, one cell, one unit, or the whole network?

Unexplained false positives

  • Pull the incubation temperature log for the flagged window

  • Confirm bottle wasn't over-filled or refrigerated

Unexplained false negatives
